The Netto Number

The Netto Number combines a risk budget chosen before an evaluation period with the adverse path realized during that period. It asks a different question from Sharpe, Sortino, or Calmar:

How much profit did this strategy produce relative to both the risk capacity it was granted and the downside it actually used?

QRT implements the calculation as q.stats.netto_number. The function is a small scalar primitive; the important work is defining its three inputs with a consistent mandate, horizon, unit, and adverse-excursion convention.

ImportantStatus of the metric

The Netto Number is a practitioner-designed framework associated with trader and author John Netto. Its economic intuition is useful, but it is not a canonical academic standard and there is no broad public evidence that it universally predicts investment skill better than established measures. Use it as an additional risk-budget-aware diagnostic, not as a replacement for return, drawdown, tail-risk, turnover, and benchmark analysis.

Origin and development

John Netto developed the modern Netto Number within his broader Unit-of-Risk (UoR) framework. His 2016 book The Global Macro Edge: Maximizing Return Per Unit-of-Risk frames investment performance around return earned under a predetermined risk budget. A chapter excerpt hosted by CQG describes the Netto Number as the most important UoR ratio and links it to the Risk Factor Compensation System presented later in the book.

The idea was subsequently discussed in public trading presentations and interviews. A 2017 MoneyShow presentation describes the measure as a way to compare returns by manager and by unit of risk. In a separate Cannon Trading interview, Netto describes evaluating a strategy, index, portfolio, or trade relative to a predetermined risk budget rather than only in nominal terms. These materials also connect the ratio to manager incentive design.

That compensation use is the historical motivation, not a restriction on the mathematics. The formula can be applied to any well-defined decision unit with an ex-ante risk budget and a realized P&L path: a trade, strategy, portfolio, model checkpoint, or reinforcement-learning episode.

The public evidence base is concentrated in Netto’s own book, excerpts, presentations, and interviews. The framework should therefore be described as a proprietary or bespoke practitioner metric, not as a finance law or a peer-reviewed theorem.

Definition

Let:

  • \(P\) be net realized profit over the evaluation period.
  • \(U\) be Unit-of-Risk: a positive loss budget fixed before the period.
  • \(A\) be maximum adverse excursion, expressed as a non-negative loss magnitude.

The framework first defines a Risk Factor as the arithmetic mean of planned risk and realized adversity:

\[ \operatorname{RF} = \frac{U + A}{2}. \]

The Netto Number is profit divided by that Risk Factor:

\[ \operatorname{NN} = \frac{P}{\operatorname{RF}} = \frac{2P}{U + A}. \]

A value of \(1\) means profit equals the blended Risk Factor. A value above \(1\) means profit exceeded it. Zero means the period broke even, and a negative value means the strategy lost money. There is no universal threshold that separates a good value from a bad one; interpretation depends on a consistently defined mandate and peer group.

WarningNegative-profit inversion

The raw ratio is not a risk-monotonic optimization objective for losing outcomes. Holding \(P\) and \(U\) fixed:

\[ \frac{\partial \operatorname{NN}}{\partial A} = -\frac{2P}{(U+A)^2}. \]

For \(P>0\), more adversity lowers the score as intended. For \(P<0\), the derivative is positive: more adversity moves the negative score toward zero. With \(P=-0.10\) and \(U=1\), increasing \(A\) from \(0.10\) to \(0.90\) changes the score from approximately \(-0.182\) to \(-0.105\). A maximizer would prefer the worse path.

This is a mathematical property of the original formula, not an implementation error in QRT. The value remains valid as descriptive reporting, but must not be the sole reward, loss, or policy-ranking criterion when outcomes can be negative.

Published-style example

For \(P=\$100{,}000\), \(U=\$1{,}000{,}000\), and \(A=\$500{,}000\):

\[ \operatorname{RF} = \frac{\$1{,}000{,}000 + \$500{,}000}{2} = \$750{,}000, \]

and:

\[ \operatorname{NN} = \frac{\$100{,}000}{\$750{,}000} \approx 0.1333. \]

import qrt as q

q.stats.netto_number(
    100_000,
    unit_of_risk=1_000_000,
    max_adverse_excursion=500_000,
)

The ratio is scale-invariant. Dividing every input by the same starting capital produces the same result:

q.stats.netto_number(
    0.10,
    unit_of_risk=1.00,
    max_adverse_excursion=0.50,
)

Both calls return approximately 0.1333.

Input contract in QRT

q.stats.netto_number deliberately accepts three explicit scalar inputs rather than a return series:

q.stats.netto_number(
    profit,
    unit_of_risk=unit_of_risk,
    max_adverse_excursion=max_adverse_excursion,
)

The contract is strict:

  • profit must be finite and may be positive, zero, or negative.
  • unit_of_risk must be finite and strictly positive.
  • max_adverse_excursion must be finite and non-negative.
  • All values must cover the same object and evaluation period.
  • All values must use the same units.

Currency amounts and fractions of starting capital are both valid. Mixing them is not. For example, a dollar profit cannot be combined with a percentage risk budget.

The function is not automatically included in q.stats.performance or q.stats.metrics. A return stream contains no authoritative record of the risk budget committed before trading, so QRT will not infer one after the fact.

Adverse excursion is not automatically max drawdown

The most important implementation choice is the meaning of \(A\).

QRT’s q.stats.max_drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough decline from a running equity high. Public Netto material also uses language such as maximum negative drawdown or adverse excursion, with examples centered on loss of starting principal. Under a principal-relative convention, giving back profit while remaining above starting equity does not increase \(A\); under standard maximum drawdown, it does.

Consider an equity path that rises from 1.00 to 1.30 and then falls to 1.05:

  • Principal-relative adverse excursion is 0.00; equity never fell below its starting value.
  • Peak-to-trough maximum drawdown is about 0.1923; equity fell roughly 19.23% from 1.30 to 1.05.

Those conventions answer different questions and can produce materially different Netto Numbers. QRT therefore requires the loss magnitude explicitly.

For a principal-relative convention on a periodic return stream:

equity = (1.0 + returns).cumprod()
profit = equity.iloc[-1] - 1.0
principal_adverse_excursion = max(0.0, 1.0 - equity.min())

score = q.stats.netto_number(
    profit,
    unit_of_risk=0.10,
    max_adverse_excursion=principal_adverse_excursion,
)

If a research mandate intentionally defines adversity as standard maximum drawdown, make that variant explicit:

score = q.stats.netto_number(
    profit,
    unit_of_risk=0.10,
    max_adverse_excursion=abs(q.stats.max_drawdown(returns)),
)

Report this as a “peak-to-trough Netto variant” rather than silently implying that it is the only definition.

Where it can be applied

The metric is not inherently a single-asset or portfolio measure. Its scope is determined by the scope of its inputs.

Evaluation unit Profit Unit-of-Risk Adverse excursion
Single trade Net trade P&L Pre-trade loss budget Trade MAE
Single-asset strategy Strategy-period P&L Strategy risk budget Strategy equity adversity
Multi-asset portfolio Portfolio P&L Portfolio mandate budget Portfolio equity adversity
Model checkpoint Validation/test P&L Fixed evaluation budget Evaluation-path adversity
RL episode Terminal account P&L Episode loss budget Episode account adversity

A raw asset does not possess an intrinsic Unit-of-Risk. AAPL, gold, or a futures contract has returns and drawdowns, but the investor, strategy, or mandate must supply the risk budget. Consequently, comparisons are meaningful only when the budgets were fixed using comparable governance.

Quant-research adaptations

Strategy and hyperparameter evaluation

The Netto Number can complement model diagnostics when every candidate is evaluated on the same data split, horizon, execution model, and risk budget. For profitable candidates it rewards profit while retaining information about the capital flexibility granted before the run. It must not be the sole ranking objective across losing candidates because of the negative-profit inversion.

A useful experiment table should retain the components, not only the ratio:

Field Why retain it
profit Distinguishes genuine profit from denominator effects
unit_of_risk Makes the ex-ante mandate auditable
max_adverse_excursion Exposes realized path stress
netto_number Provides the combined comparison score
max_drawdown Preserves conventional peak-to-trough risk
turnover and costs Detects impractical high-churn policies
tail metrics Detects risks hidden by one worst-path statistic

Do not optimize the raw ratio over many configurations and then report the best value on the same sample. Prefer a monotonic objective or explicit constraints, retain the Netto Number as a diagnostic, lock the policy and risk convention, and report once on untouched test data.

Reinforcement-learning evaluation

ImportantShould I use the Netto Number in RL?

Yes, but do not use the original Netto Number as the agent’s main reward.

Use it to measure and audit an RL agent:

  • calculate it after each episode as a performance diagnostic;
  • compare profit with both the risk budget and the adversity experienced;
  • monitor whether the agent used or breached its risk budget;
  • report it alongside return, drawdown, tail loss, turnover, and costs.

Do not use it by itself to teach the agent or select the best checkpoint. During a losing episode, a worse adverse excursion can produce a numerically higher Netto Number. An optimizer could therefore learn the wrong preference.

For training, use a reward that always prefers more profit and less risk, such as net log return with monotonic drawdown penalties, or maximize return subject to explicit drawdown and tail-loss constraints. In short: use the original Netto Number to evaluate the agent, not as its primary learning signal.

For a financial Gymnasium environment, calculate the metric from account equity rather than from the return of one quoted asset. Let \(V_0\) be starting account value, \(V_T\) terminal value, and \(V_t\) the account path. A normalized episode definition is:

\[ P = \frac{V_T}{V_0} - 1, \qquad A = \max\left(0, 1 - \min_t \frac{V_t}{V_0}\right), \]

with a fixed normalized budget \(U\), such as 0.10 for 10% of starting capital. The raw Netto Number then describes the episode under that account-level risk mandate. Do not use it by itself to rank arbitrary episodes or checkpoints.

Use it for descriptive episode reporting alongside:

  • terminal return and net P&L;
  • risk-budget utilization and breach rate;
  • peak-to-trough drawdown and drawdown duration;
  • expected shortfall and worst-decile outcomes;
  • turnover, costs, and exposure.

It is a poor direct per-step reward. The denominator changes only when a new adverse extreme occurs, the full score is unknown until the episode ends, and the negative-profit inversion compounds the usual ratio-reward incentives for inactivity, early stopping, tiny exposure, or other reward hacking.

Experimental monotonic adaptation

A Netto-inspired terminal score can retain separate planned- and realized-risk terms without dividing negative profit by risk. Let \(p_T=V_T/V_0-1\), let \(u\) be the normalized risk budget, and let \(D_T\) be a chosen non-negative drawdown measure. One candidate is:

\[ S_T = \log(1+p_T) - \lambda_N \log\left(1 + \frac{u+D_T}{2}\right), \qquad p_T>-1,\ \lambda_N>0. \]

It is monotonic in the intended directions:

\[ \frac{\partial S_T}{\partial p_T}=\frac{1}{1+p_T}>0, \qquad \frac{\partial S_T}{\partial D_T} =-\frac{\lambda_N}{2\left(1+(u+D_T)/2\right)}<0. \]

This is a QRT research adaptation, not John Netto’s formula and not currently implemented as a public statistic. Its weighting, drawdown convention, and out-of-sample value require empirical validation before it becomes an API.

For dense feedback, define running peak \(H_t=\max_{s\le t}V_s\), current drawdown \(d_t=1-V_t/H_t\), and running maximum drawdown \(D_t=\max_{s\le t}d_s\). A candidate training reward is:

\[ r_t = \Delta\log V_t - \lambda_1 d_t - \lambda_2(D_t-D_{t-1}) + \beta\,\mathbf{1}_{t=T}\tanh(S_T/\tau). \]

If account equity \(V_t\) already includes fees, spread, slippage, and financing, do not subtract those costs again. If it is a gross equity process, subtract an explicit cost term once. The terminal bonus should be treated as an ablation, not assumed to improve learning. Log-return rewards also require \(V_t>0\); the environment must define liquidation and termination behavior before equity can reach zero.

State augmentation

Drawdown and terminal trajectory scores are path-dependent. An observation containing only market features and current positions does not make their reward dynamics Markov. Include sufficient account state, for example:

\[ \left[ V_t/V_0,\ H_t/V_0,\ d_t,\ D_t,\ u,\ \operatorname{remaining\ episode\ time} \right], \]

alongside positions and market features. The budget need only be observed when it varies across episodes. This state summarizes the history needed to update the drawdown terms; more general non-Markovian objectives may require a trajectory-aware method.

When comparing RL agents, keep episode length and market windows aligned. A short policy should not appear superior merely because it had less time to encounter adversity.

For outer-loop selection, aggregate the experimental monotonic score \(S_T\), not the raw Netto Number, across a fixed panel of market episodes and random seeds. Report at least the median, lower quartile, and risk-budget breach rate. A mean alone can be dominated by a few favorable trajectories. Keep a final market window untouched by reward and hyperparameter selection.

Evaluation protocol

A useful ablation compares:

Configuration Training objective
Baseline Net log return
Drawdown additive Log return minus current/new-drawdown penalties
Original Netto Exact terminal ratio, included to expose failure modes rather than as a production default
Monotonic adaptation Dense reward plus bounded \(S_T\) terminal bonus
Constrained Return objective with drawdown and tail-loss constraints

Evaluate every configuration with metrics not all directly optimized: net return, maximum drawdown, drawdown duration, Sortino, Calmar, expected shortfall, worst-decile return, turnover, costs, budget-breach probability, regime stability, and seed stability. Explicitly probe late risk-taking during losing episodes, inactivity, episode-boundary liquidation, leverage artifacts, and frequent small gains paired with rare catastrophic losses.

Risk constraints and budget consumption

The ratio is most informative when Unit-of-Risk is a real constraint rather than a label added after observing performance. A trading environment can track principal-relative budget utilization:

\[ b_t = \frac{\max\left(0, V_0 - \min_{\tau \le t} V_\tau\right)}{U_{\$}}. \]

Here \(U_{\$}\) is the dollar loss budget. The environment may terminate an episode or force liquidation when \(b_t \ge 1\). This operational rule makes the ex-ante input auditable and prevents researchers from changing the denominator after seeing the path.

A cleaner alternative to combining every preference in one scalar reward is a constrained problem:

\[ \max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\log\frac{V_T}{V_0}\right] \]

subject to, for example:

\[ \mathbb{E}_\pi[D_T] \le d_{\max}, \qquad \operatorname{CVaR}_\alpha(-p_T) \le L_{\max}. \]

This separates compound-wealth growth from drawdown and tail-loss limits. The constraint estimator, confidence level, and enforcement algorithm still need careful validation; writing a constraint does not make finite-sample risk estimates reliable automatically.

Comparison with familiar metrics

Metric Numerator Risk denominator Ex-ante input? Main question
Sharpe Mean excess return Return volatility No Return per unit of total variability
Sortino Mean excess return Downside deviation No Return per unit of harmful variability
Calmar CAGR Peak-to-trough max drawdown No Annualized growth per worst drawdown
Recovery factor Total return Peak-to-trough max drawdown No Total gain per worst drawdown
Netto Number Net profit Mean of risk budget and adverse excursion Yes Profit relative to granted and realized risk

The ex-ante term is the distinctive feature. It is also the source of the metric’s main governance burden: a subjective or movable budget destroys comparability.

Limitations and failure modes

The 50/50 weighting is a design choice

The arithmetic mean gives equal weight to Unit-of-Risk and realized adversity. Public descriptions provide the rule, but not a general statistical derivation showing that equal weights are optimal. Researchers should treat it as a stated utility choice and test whether it adds out-of-sample information.

The budget can be gamed

A budget chosen after the result, changed during the period, or defined inconsistently across candidates makes the ratio arbitrary. Record the budget, unit, horizon, adversity convention, and breach rule before evaluation begins.

Horizon and path conventions matter

Scores from a week and a year are not directly comparable. Neither are scores based on principal shortfall and peak-to-trough drawdown. Reset rules, capital flows, leverage, fees, and whether unrealized P&L is marked to market must also match.

One path statistic cannot describe tail risk

Maximum adverse excursion records one extreme. It does not reveal loss frequency, duration, liquidity, gaps, skew, or the distribution beyond the observed path. Pair the score with expected shortfall, drawdown duration, stress tests, and simulation where appropriate.

Small denominators and inactive policies

A very small risk budget and near-zero adverse excursion can create a large ratio from modest profit. In RL, an agent may also avoid trading to protect its denominator. Always inspect absolute profit, exposure, turnover, and the two denominator components.

It is not independently validated as a universal ranking rule

The formula has clear economic intuition but a limited public empirical literature. Before using it as a production objective, test whether it improves future outcomes relative to simpler baselines such as total return under a hard risk constraint, Calmar, or a multi-objective return/drawdown criterion.

Recommended reporting standard

A reproducible Netto Number should be accompanied by:

  1. Evaluation object and exact start/end timestamps.
  2. Profit unit and treatment of fees, financing, and cash flows.
  3. Unit-of-Risk value, when it was fixed, and who or what set it.
  4. Adverse-excursion definition and whether P&L was marked to market.
  5. Starting capital and normalization convention.
  6. Budget-breach and episode-termination rules.
  7. Profit, Unit-of-Risk, adverse excursion, Risk Factor, and final ratio.
  8. Conventional return, drawdown, tail-risk, exposure, and turnover metrics.
  9. Whether the ratio was descriptive only or used in any selection objective.

With those controls, the Netto Number is a useful bridge between performance measurement and risk-budget governance. Without them, its apparent precision can hide arbitrary inputs.
